# Method and data shape

Work through the sections in order, but treat them as lenses on one problem, not a checklist to pad. Short and specific beats long and generic. Where a section below gives its own count, that count wins; otherwise 4 to 8 top-level items is usually right (sub-parts of the breakdown don't count towards that). Every range here is a ceiling, not a target: a two-sentence problem sits at the bottom of every range, and an item that restates another item is cut. The rules in SKILL.md apply throughout; they are not repeated here.

**Accepted values** (fill.py checks these, lower-case): `basis`: measured, reported, assumed, outside. `likelihood`: leading, possible, unlikely. Hypothesis `status`: open, ruled out, confirmed. `isItReal.verdict`: outside normal, inside normal, can't tell yet. Stream `who`: AI, you, client, client's staff, vendor. Stream `kind`: check, fix, decide, ask. Stream `door`: reversible, one-way. Rubric `status`: pass, fail, unknown. `factChecks[].result`: held, corrected, unchecked. `depth`: 1, 2 or 3, and the deepest level you write must equal it.

The six skill sections (`decompose`, `abstract`, `reuse`, `parallelize`, `rubric`, `evaluate`) each have a `takeaway`: one sentence with that section's conclusion, shown under its heading. Write it last, once the section is done. A section with nothing real to say is not omitted: it becomes `{ "skipped": "<one-line reason>" }` so the reader sees the gap was deliberate.

## 0. Facts first

Do this before any reasoning, because the first explanation you read tends to win.

1. **Restate** (`restated`): one sentence, how you read the problem. A misreading shows up here, in the first ten seconds, instead of at the end.
2. **Facts** (`facts`): the facts and numbers only, in a fixed order (who, what, when, where, how much, how long; several facts per slot are fine, derived figures go after the "how much" they come from), each with a `basis`: `measured` (a record you or the person have seen, including a dashboard figure the person read, even when what it measures is in doubt; say the doubt in the fact), `reported` (someone said so; a number from the client's mouth stays reported until a record is seen), `assumed` (yours) or `outside` (brought from your own knowledge; it carries a "verify:" and appears in `factChecks`). A derived figure takes the basis of its inputs. When you cannot tell whether the person read a figure or was told it, use `reported`. Quote the person's numbers exactly as given, with unit and period ("from 40 to 28 bookings a month, over 8 weeks"); show the arithmetic for any figure you derive. A fact you bring from your own knowledge goes where it first does work (a part's check, a blind spot, a rubric source) with its "verify:" there, once, "first" meaning first in the JSON; later mentions, `restsOn` included, refer to it plainly. Put it in `facts` with `basis: outside` only when the map's answer rests on it.
3. **Their theory** (`theirTheory`). If the problem statement names a cause ("because of the new site"), rephrase it as a neutral question ("Did the redesign reduce bookings?") and write that question in `theirTheory`. If the blame is general ("the newsletter isn't working", "the new hire isn't performing"), write the question about the mechanism it implies ("Are the emails reaching inboxes and being opened?"). It enters step 1 as one competing explanation with no head start, however confidently it was stated. A doubt the person implies without naming a cause ("the dashboard says X but Y happened") counts; a theory from a third party ("the host says it's your code") becomes a competing explanation labelled as theirs, not `theirTheory`. Omit the field when the person offered no cause and implied none. If the person lists fixes that have already failed, write down the premise those fixes shared; "that premise is wrong" is a candidate explanation too.

## Summary (answer first)

Three lines, max, one sentence each. Line 1: what is most likely going on; if two or three explanations are tied, say so and name them, and line 1 may then run to two sentences. "The evidence in hand cannot yet separate A, B and C" is a valid line 1 and better than a guess. Line 2: what to check or do first. Line 3: what it depends on. Write the summary last, once the explanations exist; never write a "most likely" line before the competing explanations are on the page.

Two top-level keys sit beside `summary`, not inside it:

- `confidence`: `level` is `low`, `moderate` or `high` and says how much to trust the map, which is about the evidence you had, not how likely the leading explanation is. `why` in one phrase ("built from two sentences and no records").
- `restsOn`: one to three facts that the leading explanation (or the tied front-runners) needs to be true, each with its `basis`. If one of these is wrong, the whole map moves; the reader checks these first.
- Judgments use plain words; "may", "might" and "could" are hedges that absolve the writer. Say "likely", "unlikely" or "can't tell yet".

## 1. Decompose: find the parts and how they connect

The goal is to find where the problem lives without locking onto the first explanation that sounds right. AI models tend to commit early; these steps are there to stop that.

1. **State the gap** (`gap`): one sentence describing what is observed versus what is expected, with the numbers, unit and period if the person gave any. Next to it, `impact` (what the gap costs, or "unknown") and `detection` (how and when it was noticed; a problem noticed three months late is itself a finding); both sit at the `decompose` level, as the example shows.
2. **Is it real?** (`isItReal`): leads, calls and rankings fluctuate. `verdict` is `outside normal` (bigger than the usual swings; reported figures are enough when the swing is far beyond anything seasonal, say so in `why`), `inside normal` (could be ordinary variation, seasonality, or a bounce-back from an unusually good period) or `can't tell yet` (no baseline seen and the size of the swing could be ordinary). `why` names the baseline to compare against (the same measure for the last 12 months) and says when the base is too small to tell noise from signal. If the verdict is "inside normal", summary line 1 says so and the map becomes a watch plan.
3. **Problem type** (`problemType`): how will the answer be found? `type` is one of "diagnose from data" (the records already exist, whoever holds them; say who in `why`), "find out by asking" (someone knows: the client, their staff, their customers), "find out by testing a change" (nothing recorded can tell; only an experiment can) or "choose between options". Mixed cases pick the main one and explain the mix in `why` (after the Cynefin framework).

   **When the problem is a choice** ("should we move the budget to X?"), the same sections do slightly different work. The title is the question. `theirTheory` is the preference the question implies ("Has Google Ads become a worse buy?"), or omitted when it implies none. `gap` states the choice and, if a symptom sits behind it (cost per lead doubled), the symptom; `isItReal`, `changes`, `impact` and `detection` are about that symptom, and are omitted when there is none. The parts are the dimensions the choice turns on (cost per booked job, eligibility, how much budget the option can spend). The IS / IS NOT columns are the two options, and the "distinctive" column says what a difference on that dimension means for the choice. The explanations are "A wins if..." conditions: `predicts` is what the records show if the condition holds, `explains` / `doesNotExplain` may be omitted, and two conditions may sit on the same dimension as long as their `ruleOut` evidence differs. The summary picks an option conditionally when the evidence cannot yet decide ("stay with A unless the booked jobs fell as much as the counted leads"), and names the condition that decides; for a choice, line 1 may run to two sentences.
4. **What changed, and when** (`changes`): anything that moved around the time the gap opened: the site, the ads, the tracking, a Business Profile edit, the budget, a competitor, staff, the season, a platform rule. Each with `what`, `when` and `who` ("unknown" is fine). If the person gave nothing, list the two or three changes most worth asking about, with `when` and `who` "unknown", and make asking about them a stream. Agency problems are usually triggered by a change; this is Kepner-Tregoe's step after IS / IS NOT.
5. **Map the path and the parts** (`parts`). Break the problem into the parts that together produce the result, using the client's own nouns. When the result flows through steps (a funnel, an order, a process), follow the path end to end and include the rule that moves something from one step to the next: where the counts stop matching is usually where the problem is. The parts must cover the whole problem without overlapping: name one way the result could fail that fits no part, and one that fits two; if either exists, fix the parts. Record both in `coverageTest`, each as the failure plus how you fixed the parts, in one sentence. One part feeding another is a dependency, not an overlap. Something that enters the path partway (phone calls that skip the website, walk-ins) is its own part with no prerequisite; the step where it joins lists it in `dependsOn`. A condition that colours every step rather than being a step (prices, opening hours, reputation, an unrealistic expectation) is not a part: it goes into the competing explanations, the contributing factors or the "it depends" conditions. The counting itself (the tag, the report, the rule that labels a lead) is a part when the person's numbers come through it. When the thing travels a loop (a request goes browser, server, database and back), follow the order it travels.
   - Each part gets a diagnostic **question** and a **check**: where to look, what to write down, and which direction means what. When you are not sure of a platform's exact menu, write "the X report in [platform]; confirm the path" rather than a confident guess. Ask how, what and when, not why; name who holds the record (the receptionist, the ads account), and ask for the record, not an opinion.
   - `dependsOn` lists the ids of the parts whose output this part needs; a part can need several. Leave it empty for parts with no prerequisite; those appear in the first column of the graph. No loops. An arrow you are not sure of becomes an open question, not an arrow.
6. **IS / IS NOT** (`isIsNot`, after Kepner-Tregoe problem analysis). Compare the thing that goes wrong with the closest thing that goes right, on 4 to 6 dimensions: what, who, when, where, how much, how long. On a first run with no records, 4 rows at most, and the "distinctive" column is the content: it says what each direction of difference would mean, which is what the person looks for when the records arrive. `is` and `isNot` name the two things being compared; they become the table's column headings. Each row is `{ "dimension", "is", "isNot", "distinction" }`. If the person gave no working comparison, pick the closest one that almost certainly exists (the clients who stayed, the month before, the campaign that works) and list it as an assumption. When two comparisons are equally close, use the one whose records the person most likely has as the column, and make the other a row. A cell with nothing to compare says "not applicable". For each row write what the problem IS, what it IS NOT (though it could be), and what is distinctive. Use real records if the person gave any; otherwise say what to look up and write "unknown". Never fill a cell with a guess presented as fact. Don't assume the direction of a difference: in the "what's distinctive" column, say what it would mean if the IS side were higher and what it would mean if it were lower (longer or shorter, earlier or later, more or fewer); for a row that compares kinds rather than amounts, say what it would mean if the two sides differ and if they match. One clause each way is enough. A number the person offers as the cause is one angle; find two independent indicators before leaning on it.
7. **Competing explanations** (`hypotheses`, after Analysis of Competing Hypotheses). List 3 to 6 plausible explanations in one pass, before judging any. Include at least one that goes against the most obvious answer, the person's own theory from step 0, and, whenever the problem is a number on a dashboard, "the counting changed, not the business" (a lapsed tracking number, form notifications in spam, a removed tag, a changed attribution window, bot traffic) with its own check: send a test enquiry end to end. Three variations of one explanation count as one; each must point at a different part. For each:
   - `predicts`: what the records will show if this is the cause ("the counted calls will be mostly under 30 seconds"). If you cannot write this, the explanation is a vibe: sharpen it or drop it.
   - `explains` and `doesNotExplain`: which of the facts it covers and which it leaves out. A few words each when the facts are few.
   - `ruleOut`: the evidence that would eliminate it. No two explanations may share the same `ruleOut`; if they do, they are one explanation written twice. One record may rule out several explanations, as long as each predicts something different in it.
   - `cheapestCheck`: the cheapest way to get that evidence, with where to look and what to write down.
   - `probability`: a rough percentage, all explanations adding to 100. Above 70 needs evidence in hand, not reasoning alone. When no number is honestly better than another, spread them evenly and say so in the summary.
   - `likelihood`, set from the probabilities: `leading` for the one or two explanations that sit ahead of the rest by 10 points or more, even when they are under 40 and tied with each other (then the summary says "one of two things"); `possible` for the rest that could be; `unlikely` for those that would need something surprising to be true. When three or more sit within 10 points of the top, none is leading, all of them are `possible`, and the summary names them as tied.
   - `danger: true` on an explanation that is cheap to miss and costly if true: the definition governs, not the examples (a suspended Business Profile, a manual penalty, a hacked site, a lapsed account, a bot giving customers wrong refund answers, a confirmation email that never arrives). It is checked in wave 1 regardless of likelihood.
   - On a re-run, `status` (`open`, `ruled out`, `confirmed`) and `evidence` (what the person sent that decided it). Ruled-out explanations stay in the file and collapse to one line in the report.
8. **Contributing factors** (`contributingFactors`). Problems often have several causes that only produce the failure together. Name them when the leading explanations need more than one condition to be true.

**Levels.** The person can ask for a depth of 1, 2 or 3 (default 1 on a first run with no records, 2 when records were attached); write it in `depth`. Level 1 is the parts above. Level 2 gives a part 2 to 4 `children` only where the children have checks of their own that differ from the parent's; a part whose check is already concrete has none. Level 3 breaks those children down once more, only where it helps; stop where the cause is something the client can change and the check is concrete. A child that restates its parent in smaller words is cut. Children have `name`, `question` and `check`, and their own `children` at level 3. Only level-1 parts have `id` and `dependsOn`; the graph shows level 1, and the report has buttons to show levels 1, 2 or 3, opening on `depth`.

Done when: every part has a check that says where to look; you named one failure that fits no part and one that fits two, and fixed both; every explanation has a `predicts` and a distinct `ruleOut`; the probabilities add to 100.

## Unknown unknowns: blind spots

Separate from open questions. Open questions are things the person knows they don't know. Unknown unknowns are things they probably don't know to ask about: platform rules and limits, legal or privacy requirements, technical traps, industry norms, how the client's customers actually behave. Give 3 to 5, specific to this problem, each with `area`, `whyItMatters` and `howToFind` (a concrete way to check, or who to ask). No generic warnings like "consider security": if you can't say what to check, leave it out.

## 2. Abstract: keep what matters

1. **Step back** (`stepBack`, after step-back prompting and Meadows' systems thinking). Write the measure being used to judge success (`measure`) next to the actual goal (`goal`), then in `gap` name who or what could satisfy the measure without serving the goal. Many problems are a measure rewarding the wrong thing.
2. **Keep what tells the explanations apart.** Split the details into **matters now** and **set aside for now**, each with a **why**. A detail matters now if cutting it would change which explanation leads; set aside the rest, and say what would bring it back.
3. **Come back down.** Every item under "matters now" gets a `lookAt`: the concrete record, example or test that makes it checkable. Abstraction that never returns to real records is how a wrong assumption survives.

Done when: every "matters now" item has a `lookAt`.

## 3. Reuse: call what exists

Find the steps in this work that will repeat: for this client next month, or for other clients. For each (`step`), say where it repeats (`repeatsWhere`), whether it is worth turning into a reusable skill, a script or a saved checklist (`makeSkill: true`), and `why`. True only when you can name the second occurrence; "next month, this client" counts, so does a second client the person named, and so does a conditional repeat when you name the condition in `why`. One occurrence is hypothetical. Say false when a step is one-off or changes too much each time.

## 4. Parallelize: run independent work

Group the next actions into 3 to 8 **work streams**; a check another stream already covers is not a stream. A stream that needs another stream's result lists it in `waitsFor`; everything else can start now. The graph shows waves: wave 1 starts in parallel, wave 2 waits for wave 1, and so on. Each stream has:

- `brief`: one line, containing only what that stream needs. A wave-2 stream says what it takes from wave 1, not just that it waits. Test: could a junior run it with no other context?
- `who`: who does it. `you` is the person asking, including their own staff when the problem is their own business; `client` and `client's staff` only when the problem belongs to someone else; `vendor` for a host, a platform or a tool's support; `AI` when an agent can do it alone: reading public pages, working a file that an earlier stream returns, or arithmetic. When access to an account is needed and you cannot tell whether the agent has it, write `you`. One owner per stream; when two parties are needed, split the stream by owner. This is where the human is in the loop; say it on every stream.
- `kind`: `check` (get a record or run a test; when the record is another party's, the owner is that party and the asking goes in your next action), `fix` (change something), `decide` (choose; always a human's, the client's when it is their money or their customers, yours when it is your method) or `ask` (a question only a person can answer, where no record exists).
- `door`: `reversible` (can be taken back within a day at no cost; a redirect you can remove, a setting you can restore) or `one-way` (can't be taken back, spends money, or customers will have seen it). A one-way stream never sits in wave 1; it waits for the checks that justify it. A `decide` stream is `reversible` by itself (deciding changes nothing); the fix that follows it carries the door. A check that costs a click or two (a test call through the ad) is `reversible`; say the cost in the brief.
- `returns`: what the stream must come back with: a number, a yes/no, a record.
- On fix streams only, `failsIf`: the single most likely reason the fix fails three months on, and the guard against it. When the fix depends on which explanation wins, write one fix stream per candidate fix, each with its own door and `failsIf`, all waiting for the decision; only one will run. The cap of 8 streams wins: at most two fix streams on a first run, merged by owner, with the brief saying "only the part the decision picked". A fix that "might help" is a hypothesis, not a fix stream.

Done when: every stream has `who` and `door`, and every one-way stream waits for something. fill.py does not count streams; merge, don't pad.

## 5. Build a rubric: decide what passes

Turn credible standards into checkable criteria, each bearing on this problem's result; an exposure you noticed along the way (a privacy rule, a lapsed licence) belongs in the blind spots, not here. Three criteria on a first run, led by the one the person's own words imply; add the rest when targets are agreed. For each: the **criterion**, its **source**, what **pass** and **fail** look like, and how to measure it (the `measure` field). A source must already exist: the client brief, platform documentation (the platform's own page, not a post about it), the person's own data, or a target the client has already agreed. The source has to cover the target, not just the measurement: "bookings back to the pre-launch level" measured from the client's own records is still "Preference, no source" if nobody agreed that level; so is "9 in 10 clicks from inside the service area" when the client defined the area but not the 9 in 10. Label it so and add the agreement to the open questions. A criterion anyone would expect ("the form submits without an error") still says "Preference, no source" unless a document states it; that is deliberate, and the person can add the source. "Preference, no source" may carry a short note in brackets ("Preference, no source (the 10% is ours)"). `sourceUrl` is optional; add it only for a page you are confident exists. A help centre's front page plus "verify: the X page" is fine when you are not sure of the exact page. `status` is `pass`, `fail` or `unknown`; use `unknown` unless the person gave you evidence, and a `pass` or `fail` carries `evidence` (the record that decided it). Write the evidence before the label.

## 6. Evaluate and improve: test, correct, retest

- **tests**: what to run or check (`test`), `how`, `expect` (what you expect to see if the leading explanation holds, written before the result is known), and `when` (before launch, weekly, after each change; say how long before you can tell, because "weekly" is a cadence, not a finish). Flag exactly one test `signal: true`: the single check that stays red while the problem exists and goes green when it is gone. If no record can show it, say so, and the problem type becomes "find out by testing a change".
- **itDepends**: the conditions that would change the answer ("It depends engineering"). For each `condition`, what to do if it is true (`ifTrue`) and if it is false (`ifFalse`). These are the deciding conditions, not general risks.

## Assumptions, open questions, next action

- **assumptions**: everything you assumed instead of asking, each as `{ "assumption", "ifWrong" }`. `ifWrong` says what moves in the map if the assumption is wrong. Put the linchpins first: the ones that would flip the verdict.
- **openQuestions**: what only the person or client can answer, each as `{ "question", "whoCanAnswer", "ourGuess", "changesWhat" }`, ordered by how much the answer would change the verdict; the first one is the question the chat reply names. Three to five; the rest are housekeeping and go in assumptions. `ourGuess` is the answer the map assumed meanwhile, so the person can confirm in a word. Anything you could look up yourself is a `check`, not a question; something the person already knows and did not say (which state the client is in) is a question with `whoCanAnswer: "you"`. One idea per question, never compound.
- **nextAction**: `{ "action", "owner", "by", "rulesOut" }`. One concrete action, doable today, by a named owner. Pick the check that rules out the most explanations at once, not merely the cheapest, and name in `rulesOut` which explanations it would eliminate. When that check needs someone else's records, today's action is requesting them, naming exactly what to send, plus the one check you can do meanwhile. It is usually the first step of summary line 2, made specific: who does what, where.
- **revisions** (re-runs only): `[{ "date", "changed" }]`, one line per run, so the person sees what moved.

## Data shape

Write one JSON object in this shape. Required: `title`, `summary`, `decompose.parts`, `factChecks` (the list from SKILL.md step 4; empty if you brought no outside facts) and `coverageTest` (the one-fits-none / one-fits-two check from step 5); the six skill sections are each present or `{ "skipped": "<reason>" }`; everything else is optional, and every field named in this file is drawn by the report template. `title` names the symptom (or, for a choice, the question), never the suspected cause. `factChecks` and `coverageTest` are drawn in the report under "How this map was checked". `problem` is the problem in one sentence as the person gave it, `client` names whose problem it is, `date` is today.

The full worked example is in [data-shape.md](data-shape.md); load it only when you start writing the JSON (step 3 of SKILL.md). It is about an unrelated business on purpose and shows the format only: do not reuse its parts, causes or conclusions.
