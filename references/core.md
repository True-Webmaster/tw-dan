# Core: what every map does

Work through the sections in the order below; the summary and the takeaways are written last. Where a section gives its own count, that count wins; otherwise 4 to 8 top-level items (sub-parts of the breakdown don't count). The lower number of a range is the floor and the upper a ceiling, not a target: cut an item that restates another, but keep going until every open question that would change the next action has a check. The mode file loaded with this one ([diagnose.md](diagnose.md), [choose.md](choose.md) or [build.md](build.md)) says what each section means for that kind of task and governs where the two differ.

**Accepted values** (write them exactly so, lower-case): `basis`: measured, reported, assumed, outside. `likelihood`: leading, possible, unlikely. Hypothesis `status`: open, ruled out, confirmed. `isItReal.verdict`: outside normal, inside normal, can't tell yet. Stream `who`: AI, you, client, client's staff, vendor. Stream `kind`: check, fix, decide, ask. Stream `door`: reversible, one-way. Rubric `status`: pass, fail, unknown. `factChecks[].result`: held, corrected, unchecked. `depth`: 1, 2 or 3, and the deepest level you write must equal it.

The six skill sections (`decompose`, `abstract`, `reuse`, `parallelize`, `rubric`, `evaluate`) each have a `takeaway`: one sentence with that section's conclusion, shown under its heading. Write it last, once the section is done. A section with nothing real to say (never `decompose`) is not omitted: it becomes `{ "skipped": "<one-line reason>" }` so the reader sees the gap was deliberate.

## 0. Facts first

Do this before any reasoning, because the first explanation you read tends to win.

1. **Restate** (`restated`): one sentence, how you read the problem.
2. **Facts** (`facts`): the facts and numbers only, in a fixed order (who, what, when, where, how much, how long; several facts per slot are fine, derived figures go after the "how much" they come from), each with a `basis`: `measured` (a record you or the person have seen, including a dashboard figure the person read, even when what it measures is in doubt; say the doubt in the fact), `reported` (someone said so; a number from the client's mouth stays reported until a record is seen), `assumed` (yours) or `outside` (brought from your own knowledge; it carries a "verify:" and how to verify it, once, where it first appears, and goes in `factChecks`; a named law or platform rule counts as a source once it does). A fact that depends on country or state takes its region from the request only: put that region, or "region not stated", in the assumptions. A derived figure takes the basis of its inputs. When you cannot tell whether the person read a figure or was told it, use `reported`. Quote the person's numbers exactly as given, with unit and period ("from 40 to 28 weekend orders a week, over 8 weeks"); show the arithmetic for any figure you derive. A fact you bring from your own knowledge goes where it first does work (a part's check, a blind spot, a rubric source) with its "verify:" there, once, "first" meaning first in the JSON; later mentions, `restsOn` included, refer to it plainly. Put it in `facts` with `basis: outside` only when the map's answer rests on it.
3. **Their theory** (`theirTheory`): the cause, preference or approach the person already has in mind, rephrased as a neutral question. The mode file says what counts as their theory and gives examples. It enters the competing explanations as one of them with no head start, however confidently it was stated; no handicap either, so if the evidence favours it, say so. A theory from a third party outside the business ("the delivery company says it's your packaging") becomes a competing explanation labelled as theirs, not `theirTheory`; a third party's denial ("it can't be the app") attaches to the explanation it denies, as "the vendor denies this one"; a theory from the person's own staff counts as `theirTheory`. Omit the field when the person offered none and implied none, unless the mode file says otherwise.
4. **Talk it through** (`walkthrough`). The questions come from this problem, not from a list, and from the three vantages the mode file names. Quick path: take the three in turn yourself; thorough path: team.md. Each kept entry carries its `vantage` tag from the mode file (`self` is accepted on the solo path when one vantage is not worth separating from the others).
   - **Round 1:** write six to eight questions about this problem. A good question names something from it (a person, a record, a step, a number), can be answered by imagining one real instance from start to finish, and would change at least one explanation if the answer went the other way. "Is the process set up correctly?" fails that test; "What does the baker do with an order that arrives at 9 pm Friday, after the list was printed?" passes.
   - **Answer each one** in two or three sentences (at least 15 words), as if you were standing there watching one instance happen. Say what you would see, who is involved, and what gets written down. Mark every detail you supplied yourself ("if the list is printed on Thursday, as she says"), so an imagined scene never turns into a fact later, and say how a different answer would change what you check. A fact from your own knowledge that first surfaces here carries its "verify:" here.
   - **Round 2:** read your own answers and ask of each: what does it assume, what else would produce the same thing, and what would make its check impossible to run? Write the two or three questions that raised which you had not thought of, and answer those too.
   - Each entry is `{ "round": 1 | 2, "vantage", "question", "answer" }`. Only then go on to the explanations; most of them should trace back to an answer here.

## 1. Decompose: find the parts and how they connect


1. **State the gap** (`gap`, with `impact` and `detection` beside it, all three at the `decompose` level as the example shows). One sentence each; the mode file says what they hold. Use the numbers, unit and period if the person gave any.
2. **Is it real?** (`isItReal`): `verdict` is one of the accepted values, with `why` naming what it was compared against and saying when the base is too small to tell noise from signal. The mode file says what the question is and what each verdict means.
3. **Problem type** (`problemType`): how will the answer be found? `type` is one of "diagnose from data" (the records already exist, whoever holds them; say who in `why`), "find out by asking" (someone knows: the client, their staff, their customers), "find out by testing a change" (nothing recorded can tell; only an experiment can), "choose between options" or "build something". Mixed cases pick the main one and explain the mix in `why`.
4. **What changed** (`changes`): each entry has `what`, `when` and `who` ("unknown" is fine); the mode file says what belongs here. If the person gave nothing, list the two or three entries most worth asking about, with `when` and `who` "unknown", and make asking about them a stream.
5. **Map the path and the parts** (`parts`). Break the problem into the parts that together produce the result, using the client's own nouns; the mode file says what a part is. When the result flows through steps (a funnel, an order, a process, a build), follow the path end to end and include the rule that moves something from one step to the next. The parts must cover the whole problem without overlapping: name one way the result could fail that fits no part, and one that fits two; if either exists, fix the parts. Record both in `coverageTest` (a top-level key, not inside `decompose`), each as the failure plus how you fixed the parts, in one sentence, with `fixed: true`. One part feeding another is a dependency, not an overlap. Something that enters the path partway (walk-ins, orders taken by hand) is its own part with no prerequisite; the step where it joins lists it in `dependsOn`. A condition that colours every step rather than being a step (prices, opening hours, reputation, an unrealistic expectation) is not a part: it goes into the competing explanations, the contributing factors or the "it depends" conditions. When the thing travels a loop (a request goes browser, server, database and back), follow the order it travels.
   - Each part gets a **question** and a **check**: where to look, what to write down, and which direction means what. When you are not sure of a platform's exact menu, write "the X report in [platform]; confirm the path" rather than a confident guess. Ask how, what and when, not why; name who holds the record (the counter staff, the order system), and ask for the record, not an opinion.
   - `dependsOn` lists the ids of the parts whose output this part needs; a part can need several. Leave it empty for parts with no prerequisite; those appear in the first column of the graph. No loops. An arrow you are not sure of becomes an open question, not an arrow.
   - `danger: true` on a part marks one that is cheap to skip and costly if skipped; the report draws it with a red border and a "don't skip" tag.
6. **IS / IS NOT** (`isIsNot`). Compare two things on 4 to 6 dimensions (what, who, when, where, how much, how long); the mode file says which two. On a first run with no records, 4 rows at most, and the `distinction` column is the content: it says what each direction of difference would mean, which is what the person looks for when the records arrive. `is` and `isNot` name the two things being compared; they become the table's column headings. Each row is `{ "dimension", "is", "isNot", "distinction" }`. When two comparisons are equally close, use the one whose records the person most likely has as the column, and make the other a row. A cell with nothing to compare says "not applicable". Use real records if the person gave any; otherwise say what to look up and write "unknown". Never fill a cell with a guess presented as fact. Don't assume the direction of a difference: in the `distinction` column, say what it would mean if the IS side were higher and what it would mean if it were lower (longer or shorter, earlier or later, more or fewer); for a row that compares kinds rather than amounts, say what it would mean if the two sides differ and if they match. One clause each way is enough.
7. **Competing explanations** (`hypotheses`). List 3 to 6 in one pass (up to 8 when merging helpers, team.md), before judging any; the mode file says what an explanation is in that mode and which ones must be included. Three variations of one explanation count as one. For each:
   - `predicts`: what you would see if this one is right; the mode file says where. If you cannot write this, the explanation is a vibe: sharpen it or drop it.
   - `explains` and `doesNotExplain`: which of the facts it covers and which it leaves out. A few words each when the facts are few.
   - `contradicts`: the one fact in hand that sits worst with this explanation, or "none yet".
   - `ruleOut`: the evidence that would eliminate it. No two explanations may share the same `ruleOut`; if they do, they are one explanation written twice. One record may rule out several explanations, as long as each predicts something different in it.
   - `cheapestCheck`: the least demanding check that genuinely separates this explanation from the others: who can run it, what must be in hand first, what each result would mean, and what to do if it cannot be run.
   - `probability`: a rough percentage from 0 to 100, all explanations adding to 100. Give a number only when you can say why it is more justified than the next; when you split one explanation into two or merge two, keep the combined weight the same. When no number is honestly better than another, spread them evenly and say so in the summary; the summary then ranks the next checks by how much each could change the decision, not by cost.
   - `likelihood`, set from the probabilities: `leading` for the one or two explanations that sit more than 10 points ahead of everything else, even when they are under 40 and tied with each other (then the summary says "one of two things"); everything that is not leading is `possible`, except `unlikely` for those that would need something surprising to be true. When nothing leads, the summary names the front-runners as tied.
   - `danger: true` on an explanation that is cheap to miss and costly if true: the definition governs, not the examples (an account the business depends on being suspended, a legal exposure, a message to customers that never arrives). It is checked in wave 1 regardless of likelihood.

**Levels.** Write the depth from SKILL.md step 2 in `depth`. Level 1 is the parts above. Level 2 gives a part 2 to 4 `children` only where the children have checks of their own that differ from the parent's; a part whose check is already concrete has none. Level 3 breaks those children down once more, only where it helps; stop where the cause is something the client can change and the check is concrete. A child that restates its parent in smaller words is cut. Children have `name`, `question` and `check`, and their own `children` at level 3. Only level-1 parts have `id` and `dependsOn`; the graph shows level 1, and the report has buttons for the levels, opening on `depth`. If nothing earns the requested depth, write the depth you reached and say why in an assumption.

## Unknown unknowns: blind spots (`unknownUnknowns`)

Separate from open questions. Open questions are things the person knows they don't know. Unknown unknowns are things they probably don't know to ask about: platform rules and limits, legal or privacy requirements, technical traps, industry norms, how the client's customers actually behave. Give 3 to 5, specific to this problem, each with `area`, `whyItMatters` and `howToFind` (all three required) (a concrete way to check, or who to ask). No generic warnings like "consider security": if you can't say what to check, leave it out.

## 2. Abstract: keep what matters

1. **Step back** (`stepBack`). Write the measure being used to judge success (`measure`) next to the actual goal (`goal`), then in `gap` name who or what could satisfy the measure without serving the goal.
2. **Keep what tells the explanations apart.** Split the details into **matters now** and **set aside for now**, each with a **why**. A detail matters now if cutting it would change which explanation leads; set aside the rest, and say what would bring it back.
3. **Come back down.** Every item under "matters now" gets a `lookAt`: the concrete record, example or test that makes it checkable.

## 3. Reuse: call what exists

Find the steps in this work that will repeat: for this client next month, or for other clients. For each (`step`), say where it repeats (`repeatsWhere`), whether it is worth turning into a reusable skill, a script or a saved checklist (`makeSkill: true`), and `why`. True only when you can name the second occurrence; "next month, this client" counts, so does a second client the person named, and so does a conditional repeat when you name the condition in `why`. Say false when a step is one-off or changes too much each time.

## 4. Parallelize: run independent work

Group the next actions into 3 to 8 **work streams**; a check another stream already covers is not a stream. A stream that needs another stream's result lists it in `waitsFor`; everything else starts now, in wave 1. Each stream has:

- `brief`: one line, containing only what that stream needs. A wave-2 stream says what it takes from wave 1, not just that it waits. Test: could a new person carry it out from what is written, with every prerequisite named and an alternative given if one is missing?
- `who`: who does it. `you` is the person asking, including their own staff when the problem is their own business; `client` and `client's staff` only when the problem belongs to someone else; `vendor` for a host, a platform or a tool's support; `AI` when an agent can do it alone: reading public pages, working a file that an earlier stream returns, or arithmetic. When access to an account is needed and you cannot tell whether the agent has it, write `you`. One owner per stream; when two parties are needed, split the stream by owner.
- `kind`: `check` (get a record or run a test; when the record is another party's, the owner is that party and the asking goes in your next action), `fix` (change something), `decide` (choose; always a human's, the client's when it is their money or their customers, yours when it is your method; every real decision is a `decide` stream, and the report lists them under "Where you decide") or `ask` (a question to a person: something only they can answer, or a request for a record another party holds).
- `door`: `reversible` (can be taken back within a day at no cost; a price you can put back, a setting you can restore) or `one-way` (can't be taken back, spends money, or customers will have seen it; when the clauses conflict, money and customers win over "restorable": a change that keeps spending the client's money while it settles is one-way). A one-way stream never sits in wave 1; it waits for the checks that justify it. A `decide` stream is `reversible` by itself (deciding changes nothing); the fix that follows it carries the door. A check with a small, known cost (placing one test order) counts as `reversible`; say the cost in the brief, and treat anything that costs more than a few pounds or dollars as one-way.
- `returns`: what the stream must come back with: a number, a yes/no, a record.
- On fix streams only, `failsIf`: the single most likely reason the fix fails three months on, and the guard against it. When the fix depends on which explanation wins, write one fix stream per candidate fix, each with its own door and `failsIf`, all waiting for the decision; only one will run. The cap of 8 streams wins: at most two fix streams on a first run, merged by owner, with the brief saying "only the part the decision picked". A fix that "might help" is a hypothesis, not a fix stream.

## 5. Build a rubric: decide what passes

Turn credible standards into checkable criteria, each bearing on this problem's result; an exposure you noticed along the way (a privacy rule, a lapsed licence) belongs in the blind spots, not here, unless the mode file makes an exception. Three criteria on a first run, led by the one the person's own words imply unless the mode file says otherwise; add the rest when targets are agreed. For each: the **criterion**, its **source**, what **pass** and **fail** look like, and how to measure it (the `measure` field). A source must already exist: the client brief, platform documentation (the platform's own page, not a post about it), the person's own data, or a target the client has already agreed. The source has to cover the target, not just the measurement: "orders back to the spring level" measured from the client's own records is still "Preference, no source" if nobody agreed that level; so is "9 in 10 orders ready by the chosen time" when the client set the time slots but not the 9 in 10. Label it so and add the agreement to the open questions. A criterion anyone would expect ("the order form accepts an order") still says "Preference, no source" unless a document states it; that is deliberate, and the person can add the source. "Preference, no source" may carry a short note in brackets ("Preference, no source (the 10% is ours)"). `sourceUrl` is optional; add it only for a page you are confident exists. A platform page you did not open this session still counts when you are confident it exists. Documents you have not seen and the person did not mention (a signed proposal, a contract) do not exist for this map. If you know a source but not its exact figure, write "the limit in [source]" rather than a number. A help centre's front page plus "verify: the X page" is fine when you are not sure of the exact page. `status` is `pass`, `fail` or `unknown`; use `unknown` unless the person gave you evidence, and a `pass` or `fail` carries `evidence` (the record that decided it). Write the evidence before the label.

## 6. Evaluate and improve: test, correct, retest

- **tests**: what to run or check (`test`), `how`, `expect` (what you expect to see if the leading explanation holds, written before the result is known), and `when` (before launch, weekly, after each change; say how long before you can tell, because "weekly" is a cadence, not a finish). Flag exactly one test `signal: true`: the check that most directly reflects the goal, with when its result becomes readable; say in `expect` what an unreadable or mixed result would mean. If no record can show it, say so.
- **itDepends**: the conditions that would change the answer. For each `condition`, what to do if it is true (`ifTrue`) and if it is false (`ifFalse`). These are the deciding conditions, not general risks.

## Assumptions, open questions, next action

- **assumptions**: everything you assumed instead of asking, each as `{ "assumption", "ifWrong" }`. `ifWrong` says what moves in the map if the assumption is wrong. Put the linchpins first: the ones that would flip the verdict.
- **openQuestions**: what only the person or client can answer, each as `{ "question", "whoCanAnswer", "ourGuess", "changesWhat" }`, ordered by how much the answer would change the verdict; the first one is the question the chat reply names. Three to five; the rest are housekeeping and go in assumptions. These caps hold even when a judge asks for more: a sixth question goes to assumptions, an explanation past the cap folds into its nearest neighbour. `ourGuess` is the answer the map assumed meanwhile, so the person can confirm in a word. Anything you could look up yourself is a `check`, not a question; something the person already knows and did not say (which day the list is printed) is a question with `whoCanAnswer: "you"`. One idea per question, never compound.
- **nextAction**: `{ "action", "owner", "by", "rulesOut" }`. One concrete action, doable today, by a named owner; the mode file says what kind. Pick the check that rules out the most explanations at once, not merely the cheapest, and name in `rulesOut` which explanations it would eliminate. When that check needs someone else's records, today's action is requesting them, naming exactly what to send, plus the one check you can do meanwhile. It is usually the first step of summary line 2, made specific: who does what, where.
- **builtBy**: `"team"` or `"solo"`. The team-only fields `judgedBy` and `review` are in team.md; `revisions` is in reply.md.

## Summary (answer first)

Three lines, max, one sentence each. Line 1: the answer, as the mode file defines it; if two or three explanations are tied, say so and name them, and line 1 may then run to two sentences. "The evidence in hand cannot yet separate A, B and C" is a valid line 1 and better than a guess. Line 2: what to check or do first. Line 3: what it depends on. Write the summary last, once the explanations exist; never write a "most likely" line before the competing explanations are on the page.

Two top-level keys sit beside `summary`, not inside it:

- `confidence`: `level` is `low`, `moderate` or `high` and says how much to trust the map, which is about the evidence you had, not how likely the leading explanation is. `why` in one phrase ("built from two sentences and no records").
- `restsOn`: one to three facts that the leading explanation (or the tied front-runners) needs to be true, each with its `basis`. If one of these is wrong, the whole map moves; the reader checks these first.

## Data shape

Write one JSON object in this shape. Required: `title`, `summary`, `builtBy`, `decompose` (never skipped) with `depth`, `parts` (unique ids, each with a `check`) and at least three `hypotheses`, `walkthrough` (at least six round-1 and two round-2 entries), `factChecks` (the list from SKILL.md's fact-check step; empty if you brought no outside facts), `coverageTest` (the one-fits-none / one-fits-two check from "Map the path and the parts", with `fixed: true`) and `nextAction`; the other five skill sections are each present or `{ "skipped": "<reason>" }`; every stream has `who`, `kind` and `door`; everything else is optional. Parts and streams carry `id` and `name`; `dependsOn` and `waitsFor` list ids. The section arrays are `decompose.parts`, `decompose.hypotheses`, `decompose.isIsNot.rows`, `abstract.mattersNow` and `abstract.setAside` (each `{ "item", "why", "lookAt" }`), `reuse.steps`, `parallelize.streams`, `rubric.criteria`, `evaluate.tests` and `evaluate.itDepends`. The mode file says what `title` names; it is never the suspected cause. `walkthrough` is drawn in the report under "Thinking it through"; `factChecks`, `coverageTest`, `builtBy` and `review` under "How this map was checked". `problem` is the problem as the person gave it (one or two sentences, their words), `client` names whose problem it is, `date` is today.
